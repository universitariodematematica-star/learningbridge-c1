A Study Space for Everyone

When the new academic year began, students at Riverside University noticed that many classmates had nowhere quiet and comfortable to study between classes. The library was often full, the cafeteria was noisy, and the few empty classrooms were not always open. A group of students decided to ask the university for a room that they could turn into a shared study space.

The university offered them an unused room on the second floor of an old building near the library. It was large enough for twenty people, but it had not been used for several years. The walls were pale grey, the windows were dirty, and an old wooden table stood in the middle of the room. There were also three broken chairs, two dusty metal shelves, and a narrow cupboard. The room had good natural light, but it felt cold and unwelcoming.

The students formed a small committee to plan the project. Ana, a mathematics student, was responsible for the budget. David, who studied languages, collected ideas from other students. Sofia, an architecture student, prepared a simple plan of the room. They agreed on one important rule: the space had to be useful for different kinds of learners, not just attractive in photographs.

Before buying anything, David asked students what they needed. Some wanted large tables for group projects; others preferred small desks where they could work alone. Several students asked for comfortable chairs because they spent long hours on campus. A few students also explained that they needed a quiet corner for reading and listening activities. One student who used a wheelchair suggested leaving wide spaces between the furniture so that everyone could move around easily.

The committee made a list and compared prices in three local furniture shops. They found a beautiful large round table, but it was too expensive and would take up too much space. A cheaper rectangular table was more practical, so they chose it instead. They also bought six comfortable blue chairs and two simple wooden desks. Ana checked every price and kept a record of the money. The students had a limited budget, and they wanted to save enough for lighting and learning materials.

Finding shelves was more difficult. One shop offered a modern glass cabinet, but the students worried that it could break easily and would not hold many heavy books. At another store, they found two strong old metal shelves. Their paint was scratched, but the shelves were in good condition. The shop owner agreed to sell them at a low price. The committee decided to clean and repaint them rather than buy new ones. This choice saved money and reduced waste.

The group also wanted the room to feel welcoming. Sofia suggested using light green and warm white on the walls because these colors made the room seem brighter. David brought a collection of interesting English, Spanish, and French books from a student club. Another student donated a small Italian lamp with a round yellow shade. It gave the reading corner a soft light in the afternoon. A local artist offered a colorful picture of a traditional Ecuadorian market, and the students placed it near the entrance.

Not everyone agreed about the decoration. Some students wanted a modern black-and-white design, while others preferred bright colors and traditional objects. During a meeting, the committee listened to each opinion and looked again at the purpose of the room. They decided to keep the walls simple and use a few colorful objects as decoration. In this way, the room would remain calm but would not look empty.

The work took three Saturdays. On the first day, volunteers cleaned the windows, washed the floor, and removed broken furniture. On the second day, they painted the walls and repaired the old shelves. On the third day, they arranged the desks, chairs, books, and lamps. They placed the large rectangular table near the centre for group work. The two wooden desks went beside the windows, where students could read or use their laptops. The comfortable blue chairs were placed around the table, and the shelves stood against the longest wall.

Just before the opening, the students discovered a problem. The room had only two electrical outlets, and both were far from the desks. Some students needed to charge laptops, but the committee did not want cables crossing the floor because they could cause accidents. Ana contacted the university maintenance team. A technician installed additional outlets along the wall and checked that the lights were safe. The opening was delayed by one day, but the students agreed that safety was more important than keeping the original date.

When the room finally opened, the university director visited it. She was pleased to see that the students had created a useful place without spending too much money. She especially liked the repaired metal shelves and the way the furniture had been arranged. She reminded the group that a successful learning environment depends not only on attractive objects but also on good organization, respect, and cooperation.

During the first month, the room became popular. Students used it to prepare presentations, practise languages, solve mathematics problems, and study for examinations. On Monday and Wednesday afternoons, a volunteer group helped classmates with difficult subjects. At other times, students worked quietly or read books from the shelves. A notice near the door explained the basic rules: keep the room clean, speak quietly, return books after using them, and leave the desks ready for the next person.

The committee also asked users for feedback. Some students said that the room was too warm in the afternoon, so the university provided a fan. Others asked for more dictionaries and science books. The students created a donation list and received several useful books from teachers and former students. They did not buy everything immediately; instead, they chose materials that students requested most often.

At the end of the semester, the committee reviewed the project. They learned that planning had been just as important as decorating. Listening to different opinions helped them make better decisions, while comparing prices allowed them to use their budget carefully. They also understood that a room does not become inclusive simply because it has attractive furniture. People must be able to enter it, move around safely, and use its resources.

The study space was not perfect, and the students still had ideas for improving it. However, it had become more than an empty room with new furniture. It was a shared place where students could concentrate, ask for help, exchange ideas, and feel part of the university community. The project showed them that cooperation can turn a neglected space into a valuable learning environment.
